# Curated Notable Lunar Landing Sites

This document catalogues the notable lunar landing sites identified in the challenge prompt and current lunar exploration programs, covering high-priority South Pole candidate regions, commercial mission targets, international milestones, and historical Apollo baselines.

---

## 1. Artemis III Candidate Landing Regions (Lunar South Pole, 84°S – 89.5°S)

NASA identified 13 candidate landing regions for the first crewed Artemis landing. Each region represents an approximately $15 \times 15\text{ km}$ area situated in extreme polar topography where lighting and Earth visibility change drastically over small distances.

| Region Name | Approximate Coordinates | Strategic & Environmental Context |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Peak Near Shackleton** | 89.6° S, 129.2° E | High-elevation ridge directly adjacent to Shackleton Crater; offers near-continuous solar illumination during polar summer and views into deep PSR cold traps. |
| **Connecting Ridge** | 89.4° S, 222.6° E | Elevated spine connecting Shackleton Crater to de Gerlache Crater; strong solar exposure and Earth visibility. |
| **Connecting Ridge Extension** | ~89.2° S, 218.0° E | Extension of the Connecting Ridge offering alternative landing zones along the high-illumination corridor. |
| **Faustini Rim A** | 87.1° S, 67.0° E | Elevated rim feature of Faustini Crater with access to permanently shadowed crater floors. |
| **de Gerlache Rim 1** | 88.3° S, 291.5° E | Elevated crest of de Gerlache Crater with high cumulative solar visibility. |
| **de Gerlache Rim 2** | 88.1° S, 290.0° E | Secondary landing area along de Gerlache rim providing alternative slopes and approach paths. |
| **de Gerlache-Kocher Massif** | 85.8° S, 236.4° E | Large raised massif between de Gerlache and Kocher craters offering broad Earth visibility. |
| **Haworth Crater Rim** | 87.4° S, 343.0° E | High ground adjacent to Haworth Crater, one of the coldest known PSRs on the Moon. |
| **Malapert Massif** | 85.9° S, 2.9° E | High mountain (~5,000 m above surrounding terrain) facing Earth directly; exceptional Earth line-of-sight for direct communications. |
| **Leibnitz Beta Plateau** | 85.8° S, 328.0° E | Broad elevated plateau with gentle slopes and extended solar illumination periods. |
| **Nobile Rim 1** | 85.2° S, 53.5° E | Prominent ridge along Nobile crater rim; targeted for water-ice prospecting missions. |
| **Nobile Rim 2** | 84.2° S, 60.7° E | Secondary site along the outer Nobile complex offering flat landing patches with direct Earth view. |
| **Amundsen Rim** | 84.5° S, 105.0° E | Eastern polar ridge near Amundsen crater providing contrast in lighting timing compared to western sites. |

## 2. Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) Targets

NASA's CLPS initiative contracts commercial landers to deliver science instruments and technology demonstrators to the lunar surface.

| Mission | Operator | Target Landing Site | Coordinates | Status / Highlights |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **IM-1 (Odysseus)** | Intuitive Machines | Malapert A | 80.13° S, 1.44° E | Landed February 22, 2024. Southernmost landing by a commercial mission to date. |
| **IM-2 (PRIME-1 / Athena)** | Intuitive Machines | Shackleton Connecting Ridge | ~89.4° S, 222.6° E | Targeting drill demonstration to extract volatile subsurface ice. |
| **Griffin Mission 1 / VIPER** | Astrobotic | Mons Mouton / Nobile Region | 85.5° S, 31.8° E | Large flat-topped mountain near Nobile crater selected for long-duration rover traverses. |
| **Peregrine Mission 1 (Target)** | Astrobotic | Sinus Viscositatis (Gruithuisen Domes) | 36.6° N, 39.3° W | Targeted rare silicic volcanic domes in northern Oceanus Procellarum. |
| **Blue Ghost Mission 1** | Firefly Aerospace | Mare Crisium | 18.56° N, 61.81° E | Targeted smooth basaltic plain inside the Crisium impact basin. |
| **Hakuto-R Mission 1** | ispace | Atlas Crater | 47.5° N, 44.4° E | Targeted floor of Atlas crater in the northern quadrant of the nearside. |

## 3. Notable International Landing Sites

Milestone surface exploration sites from global space agencies showing geographic diversity and high-latitude exploration:

| Mission | Space Agency | Target / Landing Location | Coordinates | Historical Significance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Chandrayaan-3** *(Vikram & Pragyan)* | ISRO (India) | Shiv Shakti Point | 69.37° S, 32.35° E | First spacecraft to successfully land in the southern high-latitude region (August 23, 2023). |
| **Chang'e 4** *(Yutu-2)* | CNSA (China) | Von Kármán Crater | 45.46° S, 177.60° E | First soft landing on the lunar farside (January 3, 2019); required Queqiao relay satellite for Earth communication. |
| **Chang'e 5** | CNSA (China) | Mons Rümker | 43.06° N, 51.92° W | Returned 1.73 kg of young lunar basalts from northern Oceanus Procellarum (December 2020). |
| **Chang'e 6** | CNSA (China) | Apollo Basin (South Pole-Aitken) | 41.64° S, 153.99° W | First sample return mission from the lunar farside (June 2024); relayed via Queqiao-2 satellite. |
| **SLIM** | JAXA (Japan) | Shioli Crater | 13.31° S, 25.25° E | Demonstrated pinpoint landing accuracy (< 100 m target error) on a sloped crater flank (January 2024). |

## 4. Historical Apollo Landing Sites (Equatorial / Mid-Latitude Baseline)

These sites serve as an essential comparison baseline. Near the equator, the Sun rises up to $90^\circ$ overhead, lunar day and night are each ~14 Earth days long, and Earth is permanently high in the sky (direct line-of-sight always available). This contrasts sharply with polar conditions.

| Mission | Landing Site | Coordinates | Surface Stay Duration | Mission Highlight |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Apollo 11** | Mare Tranquillitatis | 0.674° N, 23.473° E | 21.6 hours | First crewed lunar landing (July 20, 1969). |
| **Apollo 12** | Oceanus Procellarum | 3.012° S, 23.422° W | 31.5 hours | Precision landing next to Surveyor 3 robotic probe. |
| **Apollo 14** | Fra Mauro | 3.645° S, 17.471° W | 33.5 hours | Exploration of Imbrium basin impact ejecta. |
| **Apollo 15** | Hadley-Apennine | 26.132° N, 3.634° E | 66.9 hours | First Lunar Roving Vehicle (LRV); visited Hadley Rille and Apennine mountains. |
| **Apollo 16** | Descartes Highlands | 8.973° S, 15.500° E | 71.0 hours | First mission to investigate lunar highlands. |
| **Apollo 17** | Taurus-Littrow | 20.191° N, 30.772° E | 75.0 hours | Longest crewed lunar surface stay; Harrison Schmitt became first scientist-astronaut on the Moon. |

## 5. Canadian Space Agency (CSA) Moon Exploration Initiatives
*Reference: [Canadian Space Agency - Moon Exploration](https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/astronomy/moon-exploration/)*

Canada is a core international partner in humanity's return to the Moon through the Artemis program, developing autonomous surface robotics, orbital AI servicing systems, and astronaut crew contributions.

| Mission / Initiative | Partner / Organization | Operational Region | Coordinates | Strategic Focus & Science Objectives |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Canadian Lunar Rover Mission** | CSA / Canadensys Aerospace / LEAP | Lunar South Pole | ~89.2° S, 218.0° E | Canada's flagship lunar surface mission. Carries 6 scientific payloads (spectrometers, radiation detectors, thermal imaging) to prospect for water ice inside permanently shadowed craters and survive harsh lunar nights. |
| **Artemis II Lunar Mission** | CSA / NASA | Cislunar Free-Return Trajectory | High Lunar Flyby (~10,300 km) | First crewed deep-space mission in over 50 years. CSA astronaut Jeremy Hansen flies as mission specialist on a circumlunar trajectory. |
| **Canadarm3** | CSA / MDA Space | Lunar Gateway Station | Near-Rectilinear Halo Orbit (NRHO) | Next-generation autonomous robotic system powered by artificial intelligence to maintain, repair, and inspect the Gateway station without continuous human oversight. |
